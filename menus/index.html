Menu Archaeology

Or,
A Mock Turtle Is the Thing Mock Turtle Soup Is Made From.

On April 12, 1912, guests at the Richmond County Automobile Club’s Annual Ladies’ Dinner, held at the Hotel St. George in Brooklyn Heights, might tantalize their taste buds with a caviar canapé before indulging in mock turtle soup, then move on to broiled shad, sweetbreads, and squab en casserole. The hotel would one day become New York City’s largest.

Some of these dishes appear on menus to the present day; others have evaporated. Pick one dish and find out where it came from and where it went. Try to work your way toward an explanation for these transformations.

  1. AppetizerRead the menu (opens in a new tab) and choose a dish. Write down what you understand the dish to be; note any words that seem unfamiliar.
  2. SoupFind your dish in a historic cookbook. First, search Feeding America (opens in a new tab), Michigan State University’s collection of 76 American cookbooks from the late 1700s to the early 1900s. Jot down the date and location of the earliest recipe you find; also note its main ingredients. Then cross-check in HEARTH, Cornell University’s library of home economics books and journals from 1850 to 1950. To what extent do the recipes agree? What, if anything, has changed?
  3. EntréeTrack your dish through time. Search Chronicling America (opens in a new tab), the Library of Congress’s archive of historic American newspapers through 1963. Note the earliest and latest years your dish appears; log one advertisement, menu, or recipe that mentions it. Then look for your dish on another library’s menus. Does it travel beyond New York?
  4. DessertTry to explain the change. Use a library database (opens in a new tab) or reference book to find one reason your dish rose, fell, or changed. Hit the open internet if these initial sources prove insufficient.
  5. The CheckIn three sentences, explain how and why your dish changed. Cite at least two sources in MLA style.

The Pantry

Every claim merits a second source. Stock up here!

  • Buttolph Collection of Menus, New York Public Library Digital Collections (opens in a new tab): digitized menus, including the Hotel St. George’s.
  • Menu Collection, Los Angeles Public Library (opens in a new tab): menus from restaurants, hotels, banquets, steamships, and airlines, from the nineteenth century to the present.
  • Menus: The Art of Dining, UNLV Libraries (opens in a new tab): about 1,500 digitized menus, from Las Vegas restaurants to dining on trains and ships.
  • Feeding America, Michigan State University Libraries (opens in a new tab): 76 influential American cookbooks, late 1700s to early 1900s, searchable in full text.
  • HEARTH, Cornell University Library (opens in a new tab): home economics books and journals published from 1850 to 1950, searchable in full text.
  • Chronicling America, Library of Congress (opens in a new tab): historic American newspapers through 1963, searchable in full text.
  • Menu Collection, Culinary Institute of America (opens in a new tab): digitized menus from all fifty states and more than eighty countries, including ships, railroads, and airlines.
  • Menu Collection, Johnson & Wales University ScholarsArchive (opens in a new tab): digitized restaurant menus, added on an ongoing basis.

Answers

Mock turtle soup first came to a boil in mid-eighteenth-century England, where it served as a cheaper stand-in for green turtle soup. Cooks usually made it from a calf’s head. Campbell’s sold a canned version but stopped making it prior to 1960. By the 1960s, few menus featured it.

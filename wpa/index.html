Draw It Like the WPA

Or,
You’re So Vane, You Probably Think This Art Is About You

The year: c. 1939. The artist: Lucille Chabot. The subject: a weathervane depicting an angel (presumably Gabriel) blowing a trumpet (presumably signaling the Last Judgment). Chabot made a point of painting every rivet and patch. Her work is one of 18,257 watercolors in the Index of American Design, a Depression-era project that paid artists to record American objects not at lens-point, but by putting brush to paper.

Photography would have been faster and, in a sense, “truer” to life. Why paint it?

  1. Spend some time with Chabot’s depiction (opens in a new tab). Try to spot three details that show how the weathervane was created or repaired.
  2. Why would the government pay struggling artists (opens in a new tab) to paint seemingly mundane objects like this weathervane?
  3. Select an object you can observe from less than three feet away. Sketch it in no more than two minutes. Try to capture wear and/or repair, not just shape.
  4. Write a catalog record for your object: title, maker, date, material. Which field(s) are most difficult to complete? How could you fill in these blanks?

Answers

  1. Rivets along the seams, soldered patches on the joints and legs, a trumpet made from a separate piece; streaks of green and violet allude to weathering.
  2. No, it’s not cynical: the Index existed partly to put artists to work during the Depression. Were there other reasons? Research may hold the answer.
  3. Answers will vary! Look for scratches, stains, and signs of mending, the sort of marks that make an object singular and unique.
  4. You’ll probably struggle to cite a maker or locate a date of manufacture for a pencil or a water bottle. Museums face the same challenge; labels, receipts, catalogs, and archives rise to meet it.

In 1935, Romana Javitz, head of the New York Public Library’s Picture Collection, and Ruth Reeves, a textile designer and painter, proposed a visual record of American design. The Federal Art Project took their idea nationwide that December. Until 1942, artists painted quilts, toys, tools, and weathervanes, object by object. When the project ended, the National Gallery of Art became its products’ permanent home.

Who decided which objects counted as “American design”? Whose handicrafts were painted? Whose weren’t?
